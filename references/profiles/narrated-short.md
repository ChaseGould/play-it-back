# Profile: narrated short

Evidence: proven. "The Legend of Rusty Ol' Gus" (October 2026), a
2:37 vertical episode about a real beat-up pickup, deadpan narrator,
mostly stills, 110 credits total including narrator auditions.

Use for: a story told by one narrator over pictures. Mock documentaries,
legends, explainers, recaps, "the story of" videos, a real subject from
the user's photos.

## Timing
The narration is the clock. Write the script, record the voice, then cut
every shot to the real line lengths (not the planned ones).

## Stages
All stages. Voice design (auditions) is the most important creative
decision; let the user pick by ear.

## Defaults
- Motion: about 60% still, 25% loop, 15% animated by screen time.
  Animated only for hero beats (a transformation, an action, a payoff).
- Length: 60 to 120 s; a no-cut user story can run up to 180 s.
- The narrator reads every line, including characters' quoted lines,
  unless the user wants a second voice.
- A real-photo open that morphs into the generated look, and a cut back
  to the real photo at the end, works well when the subject is real.
- An end card (for example a trading-card style "stats" card) built
  locally costs nothing and gives each episode a recurring payoff.

## Rules
- Keep the user's story wording; only TTS spelling changes.
- Deadpan delivery: statements fall at the end, punchlines lower and
  flatter, a beat of silence after each. Questions inside quotes should
  still sound like questions.
- Cars, objects, and animals act with their real parts (headlights,
  suspension, ears) unless the user wants faces.

## Lessons from use
- 2026-10: Don't animate what the story says is still (a "rolling" can
  in a parked truck); use a still.
- 2026-10: The writer's "you still run?" got flattened by QA into a
  statement; the user wanted a question. Questions in dialogue must rise.
- 2026-10: The user liked the cut with no music. Ask before adding any.
- 2026-10: Sound effects for an action line ("coughed, sputtered,
  backfired") worked best as one sound per verb, the last one loudest.
